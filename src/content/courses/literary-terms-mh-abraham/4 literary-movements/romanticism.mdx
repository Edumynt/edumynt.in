## romanticism:

**Romanticism** was a literary, artistic, and intellectual movement that originated in Europe toward the end of the 18th century and reached its peak in most areas between 1800 and 1850. As a reaction against the rationalism of the Enlightenment and the mechanization of the Industrial Revolution, Romanticism emphasized emotion, imagination, and the individual's subjective experience. The movement valued spontaneity, freedom, and the beauty of the natural world over the formal constraints and urban focus of Neoclassical literature.

The Romantic movement began in England with the publication of *Lyrical Ballads* (1798) by William Wordsworth and Samuel Taylor Coleridge, which challenged the poetic conventions of the time by using ordinary language to describe common experiences and by asserting that poetry should arise from "the spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings." Wordsworth's preface to the second edition (1800) became a manifesto of Romantic poetry, arguing that poets should write about everyday life and speak in "a selection of language really used by men."

**Key characteristics of Romanticism include:**

**1. Emphasis on Emotion and Feeling**: Romantic writers prioritized emotional intensity and personal expression over rational thought and formal structure. They believed that deep feeling was more important than logical argument.

**2. Celebration of Nature**: Nature was seen as a source of spiritual renewal, moral guidance, and aesthetic beauty. Romantic poets often portrayed nature as a living force that could teach and heal humanity.

**3. Individualism and Subjectivity**: The movement emphasized the importance of individual experience and personal perspective. The self became a central subject of literary exploration.

**4. Imagination and Creativity**: Imagination was considered the highest human faculty, capable of transcending ordinary reality and accessing deeper truths about existence.

**5. Interest in the Supernatural and Mysterious**: Romantic writers were drawn to folklore, mythology, dreams, and the inexplicable aspects of human experience.

**6. Rejection of Industrialization**: Many Romantic writers criticized the dehumanizing effects of industrial progress and urbanization, longing for a simpler, more natural way of life.

The major English Romantic poets are often divided into two generations. The **first generation** includes William Wordsworth (1770-1850), Samuel Taylor Coleridge (1772-1834), and William Blake (1757-1827). Wordsworth's poetry focused on the relationship between humans and nature, while Coleridge explored the supernatural and psychological aspects of experience in works like "The Rime of the Ancient Mariner" and "Christabel." Blake's visionary poetry combined mystical religious themes with radical social criticism.

The **second generation** comprises Lord Byron (1788-1824), Percy Bysshe Shelley (1792-1822), and John Keats (1795-1821). Byron created the figure of the "Byronic hero"—a rebellious, passionate, and often tormented character—in works like "Childe Harold's Pilgrimage" and "Don Juan." Shelley's poetry combined political radicalism with transcendental idealism, as seen in "Prometheus Unbound" and "To a Skylark." Keats developed a rich, sensuous poetic style that celebrated beauty and art, evident in his great odes such as "Ode to a Nightingale" and "Ode on a Grecian Urn."

Romantic prose writers included Sir Walter Scott, whose historical novels helped establish the genre of historical fiction, and Mary Wollstonecraft, whose *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792) became a foundational text of feminist thought. Gothic novelists like Ann Radcliffe and Matthew Lewis explored the darker aspects of human psychology through tales of mystery and terror.

**Romantic literary techniques** included the use of symbolism to convey complex emotions and ideas, the employment of myth and allegory to explore universal themes, and the development of new poetic forms that broke away from neoclassical conventions. The movement also saw the rise of the personal lyric poem and the exploration of consciousness and memory as literary subjects.

The influence of Romanticism extended far beyond literature, affecting music, painting, philosophy, and politics throughout the 19th century. The movement's emphasis on individual rights, emotional authenticity, and the value of nature continues to influence contemporary culture and literature. While Romanticism as a cohesive movement declined by the mid-19th century, its core values and aesthetic principles helped shape subsequent literary movements including Victorian literature, Symbolism, and even aspects of Modernism.
